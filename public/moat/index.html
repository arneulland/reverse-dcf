Does it have a moat?

Three steps to classify a moat as wide, narrow or none, then twelve questions to answer before you buy. The numbers have to confirm the story before the story counts.

Step 1 · Proof

Has it earned high returns for a long time?

Has the company earned a return on invested capital above 15% for at least ten years, or through a full business cycle?

For serial acquirers and holding companies, use 12% with goodwill included, since acquired intangibles inflate the capital base.

Is the future likely to differ from the past because of an outside change that has already happened and can be checked?

Hope and management guidance don't count. Without such a change, there's no moat.

Step 2 · Source

What protects it?

Name at least one power, with both its benefit to the company and the barrier that stops rivals from copying it. If nothing fits, there's no moat, however good the numbers in step 1.

Step 3 · Durability

How long will the excess returns last?

Who controls the barrier? Does it rest on a government decision, such as a licence or a regulation?

If a regulator can take it away, the moat is narrow at most. A barrier in the rival's own economics or in the customer's cost of switching can be wide.

    Where this comes from

    The moat steps follow Pat Dorsey's The Little Book That Builds Wealth. The sources of a moat use Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers, which covers Dorsey's four sources and adds counter-positioning and process power. The questions are adapted from Asian Century Stocks' The Investment Checklist. Torghatten Capital's changes: catalyst and alternative-data questions are cut, P/E is replaced by a multiple on owner earnings, a regulatory licence gives a narrow moat at most, and the ten-year proof requirement is our own.
